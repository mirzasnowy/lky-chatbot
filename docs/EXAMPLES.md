## ex1 — leadership

Q: What did he say about Asian values and the renaissance of Asian culture?

I spoke about this most directly at the Tanjong Pagar and Tiong Bahru Lunar New Year get-together (Speech at Tanjong Pagar and Tiong Bahru Lunar New Year get-together, 5 Feb 1995) [S1]. My argument there was that the revival of Asian self-confidence follows from material success, not from sentiment. For three hundred years European and later American civilisations dominated the world because they made the scientific discoveries and had the highest standard of living; their arts, foods and dress styles spread everywhere, down to the Western lounge suit and neck tie as universal dress. The Japanese were the first non-white race to match the West, and their success encouraged South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong and Singapore, which in turn moved ASEAN, China and Vietnam. East Asians now know that given time and the opportunity to trade, invest and absorb technology, they can industrialise and catch up.

From that, I said, as East Asia develops there will be a re-affirmation of Asian culture, Asian tradition and Asian values. Japanese food and dress are now admired and widely accepted; sushi bars are common in East Asia, Europe and America. And as East Asia's people master the media, Asian cartoons, documentaries and commentators will interpret world events from an Asian perspective, instead of American voices explaining them to Asians in American terms of reference. The underlying point: it is overall success which wins admiration for life styles, dress styles, food or entertainment.

On the cultural side of language I made a related point earlier: we accept English as our working language, but we want the culture, values and philosophy of life to remain our own, which requires knowing enough of our mother tongues (Address at Malay Teachers' Union Seminar, 31 May 1978) [S2].

## ex2 — geopolitics

Q: What was his view on the Hong Kong handover and US-China relations?

I spoke to this in Hong Kong just days before the handover, so my view was formed by what I saw on the ground rather than by the ceremony. Hong Kong was about to become an integral part of China, and the practical challenge for its people was to use the transition arrangements to ease its complete return into China over fifty years (Speech at the Pacific Rim Forum, Hong Kong, 27 Jun 1997) [S4]. I set out the two competing readings of that midnight: the Western media's view that a little candle of freedom would be snuffed out, and the Chinese view that a bright era was beginning, wiping out the humiliation dating from the Opium Wars (Speech at the Pacific Rim Forum, Hong Kong, 27 Jun 1997) [S3].

On America, I was blunt. The Americans were joining issue with China over Hong Kong in order to shape the future of China, not Hong Kong; their interest in 6.5 million Hong Kongers was incidental to their interest in 1,200 million Chinese (Speech at the Pacific Rim Forum, Hong Kong, 27 Jun 1997) [S3]. I noted that democracy and human rights in Hong Kong were not an issue before the Soviet Union collapsed (Speech at the Pacific Rim Forum, Hong Kong, 27 Jun 1997) [S5].

Yet I supported Clinton's deep engagement with China and expected no rupture, no embargo, because good sense would prevail on both sides (Speech at the Pacific Rim Forum, Hong Kong, 27 Jun 1997) [S1]. I warned that a US-China clash over Hong Kong or Taiwan was not tenable in the longer run and could shorten the American presence in Asia that had underpinned our stability (Speech at the Pacific Rim Forum, Hong Kong, 27 Jun 1997) [S4].

## ex3 — history

Q: Why did Singapore leave Malaysia in 1965?

I can only tell you what I said at the time, in the days immediately after it happened.

On the morning of 9 August 1965 I explained the train of events. When I first met the Tengku at 12.30 on the Saturday morning, I was still not convinced there was no other way. I believed I could still convince him that there were a number of other ways of reducing communal tensions in Malaysia, such as a looser Federation. But after I spoke with the Tengku alone, and heard what he told me, I realised there was no other way than what he thought was the solution: that we had to leave Malaysia. He had an intuition about these matters, and from what he said I knew we would all be in for big communal trouble if I and my colleagues insisted on going on with Malaysia as it was. I was frank: any other kind of Malaysia than a Malaysian Malaysia was unacceptable; you cannot get people to accept it (Transcript of press conference at Broadcasting House, 9 Aug 1965) [S4].

The pace was part of it. A highly urbanised and politically sophisticated city of two million people may have set a pace they thought too fierce, too rapid; and there were the daily outpourings in the Jawi newspapers (same transcript) [S4]. I also spoke of ideological differences between us and the former Central Government and the Alliance Government (Transcript of press conference at Broadcasting House, 9 Aug 1965) [S3].

The plain fact is that we lost our battle. We had wanted a multi-racial, tolerant society across Malaya, Singapore, Sabah and Sarawak, and we could not achieve multi-racialism and integration within Malaysia (Translation of speech at National Day Celebration Dinner, Geylang Serai, 25 Aug 1966 — a translation, not my delivered English) [S2]; (Transcript of press conference, 9 Aug 1965) [S5].

## ex4 — economy

Q: What was his economic outlook at the turn of the millennium?

At the SCCCI Millennium Celebration Dinner I set out where we stood as the century closed, and my outlook was confident but not complacent. We had come through two recessions in 34 years, 1985 and 1998, and recovered quickly both times – four quarters and two quarters respectively, V-shaped recoveries made possible by sound fundamentals and rational policies (Speech at SCCCI Millennium Celebration Dinner, 28 Dec 1999) [S1]. The key factors were three: staying relevant to the world by welcoming MNCs and upgrading from low-cost manufacturing to capital-intensive, high value-added work and regional headquarters; concentrating on human resources through education, training and skills upgrading; and looking ahead, with a prudent Government spending within its means and accumulating reserves that served as our ballast through the Asian financial crisis (Speech at SCCCI Millennium Celebration Dinner, 28 Dec 1999) [S1].

But I warned we had not "arrived". The speed at which things turned bad showed we must always be on our toes. The IT revolution and the Internet would transform global markets, and in a dynamically changing environment the danger of becoming irrelevant is high. We had to remain plugged into the global network as a node in the flow of trade, investment, knowledge and technology – keeping our markets and minds open, welcoming foreign companies and talents, receptive to new technology and ideas, redefining our roles and finding new ways to add value (Speech at SCCCI Millennium Celebration Dinner, 28 Dec 1999) [S1].

## ex5 — education

Q: What did Lee Kuan Yew say about education and nation-building?

Education, in my speeches, was never an end in itself. It was the instrument by which a disparate population becomes a nation and a citizenry fit to survive.

In 1966 I told the seminar on education and nation-building that the accent must now be on quality and not quantity — we had built the schools and trained the teachers, and now had to ask what the money bought. My answer was the good citizen: the man or woman whose natural talent has been nurtured to the maximum, fit to earn a livelihood and bring up a family. We do not grudge the $143 million, but we must get its worth returned in citizens who are robust, well-educated, skilled and well-adjusted (Opening address, seminar on 'Education and Nation-Building', 27 Dec 1966) [S1].

The mechanism of nation-building was language. At the 1959 rally of teachers I said our 10,600 teachers were the most influential group of people in Singapore, with over fifty-four per cent of the population under eighteen in their charge. If the four language streams taught four different standards of right and wrong, we would produce four different groups of people and no coherent society; hence bilingualism and eventually a common series of readers in all the main subjects (Speech on responsibilities of teachers, Education Ministry rally of teachers, 8 Dec 1959) [S3].

And the teaching itself had to be sound. In that same 1966 address I warned of teachers instructing in languages they had never mastered — the problem of a thing "three times removed" — because a capacity for accurate expression of ideas is vital to any communication (Opening address, seminar on 'Education and Nation-Building', 27 Dec 1966) [S5].

National Service I described as part of the education of citizens: every citizen has a dual role, worker and fighter (Speech at dinner in honour of National Servicemen, 15 Sep 1968) [S2].

## ex6 — governance

Q: What was his view on the press and government?

The press, in my view, is not a universal institution with one correct role. It plays a role which differs from one country to another, and when those differences are misunderstood or ignored, as frequently happens with Western media operating in developing countries, the result is friction (Address to the American Society of Newspaper Editors, 14 Apr 1988) [S1]. The American model, with the press free to publish what it chooses however irresponsible, is good for the US, but as a universal standard its applicability has not been proven (Address to the American Society of Newspaper Editors, 14 Apr 1988) [S4].

Singapore's history matters here. It was a British colony with no history of a freewheeling rambunctious press, and had the British not used press laws against Chinese-language newspapers crusading for the Malayan Communist Party in the 1950s and 1960s, the MCP would have done to Singapore what the Chinese Communist Party did in China (Address to the American Society of Newspaper Editors, 14 Apr 1988) [S1][S4]. Where the marketplace of ideas was practised in newly independent nations, the results were unhappy: confusion and dissension in Sri Lanka and India, and a wildly partisan press in the Philippines that helped Marcos declare martial law (Address to the American Society of Newspaper Editors, 14 Apr 1988) [S4].

So the line I drew is this: foreign correspondents are observers, not participants, in Singapore's domestic politics. When the Far Eastern Economic Review would neither substantiate nor withdraw defamatory allegations, I restricted circulation rather than ban, because the whole exercise is a test in wills: who has the right to publish what in Singapore (Address to the American Society of Newspaper Editors, 14 Apr 1988) [S3][S5].

On the 1965 press conference [S2], that transcript concerns Indonesia and survival, not the press's role, so it does not bear on this question.
